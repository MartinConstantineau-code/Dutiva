## [ON] Ontario — Wage deductions and pay statements

Under Ontario's Employment Standards Act, 2000 (Loi de 2000 sur les normes d'emploi), an employer may deduct from an employee's wages only where a statute of Ontario or Canada requires it (income tax, Employment Insurance premiums, Canada Pension Plan contributions), where a court order authorizes it, or where the employee has given written authorization stating a specific amount or a method of calculating it — an oral or blanket authorization is not sufficient, and the employee may revoke a written authorization for a deduction made for someone else's benefit. Employers must pay all wages earned in a pay period, other than accruing vacation pay, no later than the regular pay day, and must give a wage statement (relevé de paye) on or before pay day setting out the pay period, wage rate, gross wages and how they were calculated, the amount and purpose of each deduction, any room-or-board amounts, and net wages. When employment ends, outstanding wages and vacation pay are due by the later of seven days after the last day worked or the next regular pay day.

_Source: [Ontario.ca — Your guide to the Employment Standards Act: Payment of wages](https://www.ontario.ca/document/your-guide-employment-standards-act-0/payment-wages) — ESA ss. 11–13; verified against the live ontario.ca page on 2026-07-29._

## [ON] Ontario — Employer record-keeping and retention periods

Under Ontario's Employment Standards Act, 2000, employers must record and retain specific information for each employee. Name, address and starting date of employment must be kept for three years after the employee stops working for the employer. Dates, times and hours worked each day and week, the information on each wage statement, and copies of excess-hours-of-work or overtime-averaging agreements must each be kept for three years after the relevant work day, week, or the agreement's last day of application. Vacation time records must generally be kept for five years after the record was made. The ESA does not prescribe a record format, but records must be readily available for inspection by an employment standards officer, even where a bookkeeper or other person retains them on the employer's behalf.

_Source: [Ontario.ca — Your guide to the Employment Standards Act: Record keeping](https://www.ontario.ca/document/your-guide-employment-standards-act-0/record-keeping) — ESA Part VI (ss.15–16); verified against the live ontario.ca page on 2026-07-29._

## [ON] Ontario — Temporary layoff and recall rules

Under Ontario's ESA, an employee is on temporary layoff (mise à pied temporaire) when an employer cuts back or stops their work without ending the employment relationship; no written notice or reason is required unless a contract or collective agreement says otherwise. A layoff stays "temporary" if it lasts: not more than 13 weeks in any 20 consecutive weeks; or more than 13 but less than 35 weeks in any 52 consecutive weeks under qualifying conditions (continued substantial payments, benefit contributions, or an approved/agreed recall date); or, for unionized employees, 35+ weeks with in-agreement recall. As of November 27, 2025, an extended temporary layoff of up to 52 weeks in 78 consecutive weeks is available by written agreement, subject to Director of Employment Standards approval. A layoff exceeding these limits is deemed a termination.

_Source: [Ontario.ca — Your guide to the Employment Standards Act: Termination of employment](https://www.ontario.ca/document/your-guide-employment-standards-act-0/termination-employment) — ESA s.56(2)–(3.6); extended temporary layoff in effect since November 27, 2025; verified 2026-07-29._

## [ON] Ontario — Constructive dismissal under the ESA

Under Ontario's ESA, a person's employment is treated as terminated if the employer constructively dismisses them and the employee resigns in response within a reasonable time. A constructive dismissal (congédiement déguisé) may occur when an employer makes a significant change to a fundamental term or condition of employment without the employee's consent — a significant salary cut, or a significant negative change to location, hours, authority, or position — or harasses/abuses an employee, or gives a "quit or be fired" ultimatum. Even an otherwise-"temporary" ESA layoff can be a constructive dismissal if the contract doesn't permit laying the employee off. Ontario's guide calls this "a complex and difficult subject."

_Source: same termination-employment page — ESA s.56(1)(b); descriptive only; verified 2026-07-29._

## [ON] Ontario — WSIB workplace injury basics: reporting and loss-of-earnings benefits

Ontario workplace injuries are covered by the WSIB under the Workplace Safety and Insurance Act. An employer must pay an injured worker their full wages for the day of the injury. If the worker needs treatment beyond first aid, is absent, earns less than regular pay, or requires modified work at less than regular pay (or at regular pay for more than 7 calendar days), the employer must report to the WSIB within 3 business days and give the worker a copy. Workers who lose income may receive a loss-of-earnings benefit equal to 85% of net average earnings (for injuries on or after January 1, 1998), up to an annual maximum, paid every two weeks and reviewed annually for up to six years.

_Source: [WSIB Ontario — Loss of earnings benefit](https://www.wsib.ca/en/loss-earnings-benefit); [Report an injury or illness](https://www.wsib.ca/en/businesses/claims/report-injury-or-illness) — verified 2026-07-29._

## [QC] Quebec — Pay intervals and permitted wage deductions (retenues sur le salaire)

Under Quebec's Act respecting labour standards, administered by the CNESST, an employer has one month to pay a new hire's first wages; after that, wages must be paid at intervals of no more than 16 days (one month for managers). At each pay, the employer must give a pay slip (bulletin de paye) showing hours, wage rate, gross wages, each deduction's nature and amount, and net wages. An employer may deduct from wages only where required by law, regulation, court order, collective agreement, decree, or a mandatory supplemental pension plan; any other deduction requires the worker's written, revocable consent stating its specific purpose — except consent to a mandatory pension or group insurance plan, which cannot be revoked.

_Source: [CNESST — Pay; Pay slip](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/working-conditions/wage-and-pay/pay) — LNT arts. 43–51; verified against live CNESST pages on 2026-07-29; CNESST blocks direct HTTP fetching (403), pages read via browser._

## [QC] Quebec — Employer payroll record retention (registre de paye)

Under Quebec's Regulation respecting a registration system or the keeping of a register (made under the Act respecting labour standards), an employer must keep a registration system or register with prescribed particulars for each employee and pay period, for a three-year period per year of records. Separately, the Act respecting the Québec Pension Plan requires payroll registers to be kept at least four years after the fiscal year in which an employee was terminated, and the Civil Code of Québec's prescription period commonly extends terminated-employee record retention to at least three years from termination as a litigation safeguard.

_Source: [LégisQuébec — N-1.1, r. 6, s.2](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/rc/N-1.1,%20r.%206); cross-checked against the CNESST interpretation guide's English mirror — verified 2026-07-29._

## [QC] Quebec — Layoff (mise à pied) and recall rules

Under Quebec's Act respecting labour standards, a layoff (mise à pied) temporarily suspends the employment contract; the relationship is maintained and the worker may be recalled. At the time of a layoff expected to last more than 6 months, the employer must give written notice of termination (avis de cessation d'emploi) per the standard service-length schedule (1 week for 3 months–1 year; 2 weeks for 1–5 years; 4 weeks for 5–10 years; 8 weeks for 10+ years), or pay an indemnity instead. An indefinite or initially-shorter layoff that ends up exceeding 6 months triggers the same indemnity once the 6-month mark is reached, without itself ending the employment relationship.

_Source: [CNESST — Termination, layoff, dismissal and resignation](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/working-conditions/termination-employment/termination-layoff-dismissal-and-resignation); [Notice of termination of employment and indemnity](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/working-conditions/termination-employment/notice-termination-employment-and-indemnity) — LNT ss.82–84; verified 2026-07-29; CNESST blocks direct HTTP fetching (403), pages read via browser._

## [QC] Quebec — Constructive dismissal (congédiement déguisé)

Quebec workers employed by the same employer for at least 2 years may file a complaint if dismissed without just and sufficient cause. Constructive dismissal (congédiement déguisé) is a roundabout way of dismissing a worker by presenting the end of employment as a layoff, or by imposing significant, unjustified changes to working conditions that force a resignation. A related concept, "double sanction," is disciplining a worker twice for the same misconduct. Not every change qualifies — CNESST's own guidance gives an example of a modest schedule reduction found not to be constructive dismissal. Complaints go to the Tribunal administratif du travail, which may order reinstatement or compensation.

_Source: [CNESST — Complaint concerning dismissal without just and sufficient cause](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/client-services/complaints-recourses/labour-standards-complaints/complaint-concerning-dismissal-without-just-and) — LNT ss.124–135; descriptive only; verified 2026-07-29; CNESST blocks direct HTTP fetching (403), page read via browser._

## [QC] Quebec — CNESST workplace injury basics: employer obligations and income replacement

Quebec workplace injuries are covered by the CNESST under the LATMP. An employer must give first aid and pay for transportation to care if needed. If a worker cannot work for the rest of the accident day, the employer pays 100% of that day's wages. For absences of 14 days or less, the employer pays 90% of the worker's net wages for days they'd normally have worked (via the ADR form, reimbursed by CNESST). From day 15 onward, CNESST pays the 90%-of-net-income indemnity directly, every two weeks. Claims are due within 6 months of the injury (2 years for sexual-violence-related injuries).

_Source: [CNESST — What should you do if you have a work accident?](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/procedures-and-forms/workers/work-accident-or-occupational-disease/what-should-you-do-if-you-have-work-accident); [Income replacement indemnity](https://www.cnesst.gouv.qc.ca/en/procedures-and-forms/workers/compensation-and-reimbursements/compensation/income-replacement-indemnity) — verified 2026-07-29; CNESST blocks direct HTTP fetching (403), pages read via browser._

## [FED] Federal — Permitted wage deductions and wage recovery (Canada Labour Code)

Under Part III of the Canada Labour Code, a federally regulated employer may deduct only amounts required by federal/provincial statute, authorized by court order or collective agreement, authorized in writing by the employee (specific amount, purpose, frequency — no blanket authorization), overpayments, or amounts prescribed by regulation. Property-damage deductions need written consent, and only if no one else had access to the property. Each pay day requires a written statement of hours, rate, deductions, and net pay. The Labour Program can recover up to 24 months of unpaid wages or other amounts owed.

_Source: [Canada.ca — Pay and minimum wage, deductions, and wage recovery](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/workplace/federal-labour-standards/pay-deductions.html) — Canada Labour Code s.254, s.254.1; verified 2026-07-29._

## [FED] Federal — Employer payroll and employment record retention (Canada Labour Code)

Under Part III of the Canada Labour Code and the Canada Labour Standards Regulations, a federally regulated employer must keep an employment and payroll record for each employee for at least 36 months, plus a further 36 months after employment ends. Written employment statements (given within the employee's first 30 days) must also be kept for 36 months post-termination. Student-intern records must be kept for 36 months after the internship ends. Records must be available for examination by the Labour Program at any reasonable time.

_Source: [Canada.ca — Employer compliance with federal labour standards](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/corporate/portfolio/labour/programs/labour-standards/employer-compliance.html) — Canada Labour Code s.252(2), s.253.2(3); Canada Labour Standards Regulations s.24; verified 2026-07-29._

## [FED] Federal — Temporary layoff and recall rules (Canada Labour Code)

Under the Canada Labour Standards Regulations, a temporary layoff is not a termination where: it results from a strike/lockout; it lasts 3 months or less; it lasts more than 3 months and the employer gives written notice of a fixed recall date within 6 months and recalls accordingly; the employee keeps receiving agreed payments/benefits/SUB entitlement during a layoff of more than 3 months; the layoff is 12 months or less under a collective-agreement work guarantee; or it's 3–12 months with maintained collective-agreement recall rights throughout. Outside these conditions, the layoff becomes a termination; failing to return after a valid recall is deemed a quit (no severance).

_Source: [Canada.ca — Termination, layoff or dismissal](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/workplace/federal-labour-standards/termination.html) — Canada Labour Standards Regulations s.30(1)–(2); verified 2026-07-29._

## [FED] Federal — Constructive dismissal and unjust dismissal complaints (Canada Labour Code)

Part III's unjust dismissal provisions (s.240) apply to constructive dismissals as well as open employer-initiated dismissals. IPG-033 defines constructive dismissal as the employer failing to comply with the contract in a major respect, unilaterally changing employment terms, or expressing a settled intention to do either — "disguised dismissal" or "quitting with cause." A non-managerial employee outside a collective agreement, with 12+ consecutive months of continuous employment, may file an unjust dismissal complaint (including a constructive-dismissal one) within 90 days. The Board may order reinstatement, compensation, or another fair remedy.

_Source: [Canada.ca — Constructive dismissal (IPG-033)](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/laws-regulations/labour/interpretations-policies/constructive-dismissal.html); [Termination, layoff or dismissal](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/workplace/federal-labour-standards/termination.html) — Canada Labour Code s.240; verified 2026-07-29._

## [FED] Federal — No separate federal workers' compensation scheme for private-sector employees

Federally regulated private-sector employers don't pay into or claim from a distinct federal workers' compensation board. Federal government employees are compensated under the Government Employees Compensation Act (GECA) via the Federal Workers' Compensation Service (FWCS), with claims adjudicated by the provincial workers' compensation board where the employee usually works (Alberta's WCB for Yukon/NWT/Nunavut-based employees; Ontario's WSIB for those usually working outside Canada). The provincial board pays compensation directly. This is an absence chunk analogous to the Quebec severance one in the 2026-07-26 seed: there is no separate "federal WSIB/CNESST equivalent" — federally regulated private-sector workers are covered by the workers' compensation legislation of the province where they work.

_Source: [Canada.ca — Compensation for federal workers](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/workplace/health-safety/compensation/federal-workers.html); [How to submit a claim](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/workplace/health-safety/compensation/federal-workers/submit-claim.html) — Government Employees Compensation Act; verified 2026-07-29._
